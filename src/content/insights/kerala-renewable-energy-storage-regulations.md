summary: "In May 2025 the Kerala State Electricity Regulatory Commission issued draft Renewable Energy and Related Matters Regulations for public consultation. The draft placed energy storage — battery systems and pumped storage — at the centre of the next phase of Kerala’s renewable programme, alongside existing net metering, net billing and gross metering options. It also introduced virtual and group net metering for shared solar, virtual power plants so aggregators could take small generators and storage into the market, peer-to-peer trading, and vehicle-to-grid participation with a special time-of-use signal for peak export. The Commission said the text would be finalised only after hearing and written comments."
whyItMatters: "Kerala already has high rooftop-solar adoption and a sharp evening peak. Rules that credit daytime export but do not treat storage, group metering or peak export will keep pushing energy onto the grid at noon and drawing it back after sunset. The draft is a regulatory attempt to make storage, shared solar and flexible demand part of the same framework as net metering, rather than leaving those questions to ad hoc utility practice."

The consultation paper treated surplus daytime solar as a system problem, not only a billing problem. Storage — whether batteries or pumped hydro — was described as the means to hold energy for the evening peak. That is the same operating tension visible in other high-solar distribution networks, and it is why metering choice, banking and peak-hour treatment cannot be separated from interconnection and protection practice.

Virtual and group net metering were framed as a way for institutions and communities to share a single plant. Virtual power plants and peer-to-peer trading would, if notified, pull rooftop and storage owners closer to market participation through an aggregator or platform. Vehicle-to-grid rules, including a special time-of-use incentive for peak export, would treat EV batteries as a demand-side resource rather than only as new load.

None of those mechanisms work as a slogan. They depend on metering, settlement, hosting-capacity studies, and clear liability between the consumer, the licensee and any aggregator. That is the layer of work a consultancy assignment in Kerala now has to cover when a client asks about “solar plus storage” or shared generation.
